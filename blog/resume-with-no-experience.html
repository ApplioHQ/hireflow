How to Write a Resume With No Experience

No formal job history does not mean you have nothing to show. A good first resume makes your preparation, projects, and contribution easy to see.

November 20, 2026 · 6 min read · Applio

Start by changing the question. Instead of asking, "What jobs have I had?", ask, "What proof do I have that I can do this kind of work?" School projects, volunteer work, student organizations, caregiving, coursework, internships, and independent work can all provide that proof when they are described clearly and truthfully.

Use this section order

  1. Contact information: name, city or region, email, LinkedIn, and a portfolio if it is relevant.
  2. Short summary: one or two lines naming the target role and the strengths you can support.
  3. Education: school, degree or expected graduation, relevant coursework, honors, and GPA only if it helps.
  4. Projects and experience: class projects, internships, volunteering, freelance work, student leadership, or community work.
  5. Skills: tools, languages, and job-relevant strengths that you can actually demonstrate.

Turn activities into evidence

Do not list an activity and stop there. Explain the goal, what you did, and what changed because of your work. Even when a project did not produce revenue, it can show scope, quality, speed, collaboration, or a result.

Weak: Helped run the school club's social media.
Stronger: Planned a weekly content calendar for a 25-member club, wrote posts for three events, and coordinated approvals with club officers.

Use numbers when they are factual: team size, number of events, users interviewed, pages researched, files organized, or time saved. Do not invent a metric to make a bullet sound stronger.

Match the role, not every possible role

Read the job description and select examples that connect to its real requirements. A customer-facing job may value communication, reliability, and problem solving. An analyst internship may value research, spreadsheets, and presenting findings. Reorder your bullets so the most relevant proof appears first.

For a reality check on a target occupation, use O*NET OnLine to review common tasks, skills, and tools. Use that research to identify what you can honestly demonstrate, not to add skills you have never used.

What to leave out

  • Generic soft-skill lists with no example behind them.
  • Paragraphs that explain why you lack experience.
  • Photos, graphics, and dense design that make the document harder to scan.
  • Irrelevant personal details, including age, marital status, or a full street address.

Before you apply

Keep the resume to one page, proofread it aloud, and ask whether each bullet helps a recruiter understand your readiness for this specific role. Then compare it with the job description using an ATS check and only add missing terms when they accurately describe your work.

Source note: The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that volunteering and student internships can provide relevant experience for occupations. See its Occupational Outlook Handbook methodology and Applio's editorial policy.
