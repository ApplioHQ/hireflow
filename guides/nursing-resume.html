Nursing Resume: The Complete Guide for 2026

Updated 2026-11-25 · 14 min read · Applio
A strong nursing resume uses reverse chronological format, leads with your license and certifications, and turns every bullet into a measurable patient outcome. Include your RN license number, BLS/ACLS certifications, EMR proficiency (Epic, Cerner), and unit-specific skills. 1. What hiring managers look for in a nursing resume

Nurse recruiters scan hundreds of resumes per opening. They look for three things in the first 10 seconds:

  • Active, unencumbered license in the state (or a compact license). If your license type and number are not immediately visible, some ATS systems reject the application outright.
  • Relevant clinical experience matched to the unit. A med-surg posting wants med-surg bullets, not a generic list of nursing duties. Specialties like ICU, ER, L&D, and OR are not interchangeable in a recruiter's eyes.
  • Certifications that match the posting. BLS is table stakes. ACLS, PALS, TNCC, or specialty certs (CCRN, CEN, RNC-OB) are differentiators that ATS filters actively screen for.

Beyond the basics, hiring managers want to see patient outcomes, not task lists. "Administered medications" tells them nothing they don't already know. "Maintained 99.2% medication administration accuracy across a 6-patient assignment on a 36-bed telemetry unit" tells them you are precise, can handle volume, and understand the unit they are hiring for.

2. Best format and layout for nurses

Use reverse chronological format. It is what every hospital recruiter expects, and it is what ATS software parses best.

Section order

  1. Header (name, credentials after name like BSN/RN, phone, email, city/state, license number)
  2. Professional summary (3-4 sentences)
  3. Licenses and certifications (put this high because recruiters check it first)
  4. Clinical experience (reverse chronological)
  5. Education
  6. Skills (ATS keyword section)

Notice that licenses and certifications come before experience. In most industries, education goes near the bottom. In nursing, your credentials are a hard requirement, so surface them early.

Length

One page if you have under 10 years of experience. Two pages if you have 10+ years, hold multiple specialties, or have extensive certifications and committee work. New grads should always stay on one page.

Formatting rules for ATS

  • No tables, text boxes, columns, or graphics in the body
  • Standard section headers: "Clinical Experience" or "Work Experience," not creative labels like "Where I've Healed"
  • Use a standard font (Calibri, Arial, Times New Roman) at 10-11pt
  • Save as .docx or PDF depending on what the posting requests

3. Write your nursing summary

Your summary sits at the top of the resume and gives the recruiter a compressed version of your clinical profile. Aim for 3-4 sentences and 50-80 words. Include your license type, years of experience, primary specialty, one measurable accomplishment, and what you are looking for.

Example (experienced RN):
Registered Nurse (BSN, RN) with 5 years of medical-surgical and telemetry experience across a 420-bed Level II trauma center. Managed 6-patient assignments while maintaining a 98.5% medication administration accuracy rate and a unit-low 1.2% patient fall rate. ACLS and NIH Stroke Scale certified. Seeking a charge nurse or clinical lead role in an acute care setting.
Tip: mirror the language from the job posting. If they say "patient-centered care," use that phrase. If they say "interdisciplinary collaboration," work it into your summary.
Example (new grad):
BSN-prepared Registered Nurse with 720+ clinical hours across medical-surgical, pediatric, and emergency department rotations at [Hospital Name]. Trained in Epic EMR, IV therapy, wound care, and patient assessment. Earned departmental recognition for a hand-hygiene compliance project that improved unit rates from 81% to 96%. Seeking a residency position in acute care.

4. Experience section: before-and-after bullet rewrites

The experience section is where most nursing resumes fall flat. Nurses tend to list duties instead of outcomes. Here is the formula:

[Action verb] + [what you did, with specifics] + [measurable outcome or scope]

Before and after examples

Before: Provided patient care on a medical-surgical unit.

After: Managed care for a 6-patient assignment on a 36-bed medical-surgical unit, maintaining 98% medication administration accuracy and coordinating discharge planning with case management for an average of 4 discharges per shift.
Before: Helped train new nurses on the unit.

After: Precepted 8 new graduate nurses through a 12-week orientation program, with 100% retention at the 6-month mark and an average time-to-independent-practice of 10 weeks.
Before: Monitored patients on telemetry.

After: Interpreted cardiac rhythms and monitored 4-6 telemetry patients per shift, identifying 3 critical arrhythmias over 6 months that led to rapid response activations and prevented adverse outcomes.
Before: Assisted with wound care.

After: Performed wound assessments and dressing changes for 15+ post-surgical patients weekly, reducing wound infection rates on the unit by 22% over two quarters through standardized documentation and early intervention protocols.

Action verbs for nursing resumes

Patient care: Assessed, Monitored, Administered, Triaged, Managed, Coordinated
Leadership: Precepted, Mentored, Led, Supervised, Delegated, Chaired
Quality: Reduced, Improved, Implemented, Standardized, Achieved, Maintained
Documentation: Documented, Charted, Reported, Tracked, Audited

5. Must-have skills and keywords

ATS systems scan for exact keyword matches. The skills section is where you load the terms the job posting uses. Group them by category for readability.

Clinical skills

Patient assessment, Medication administration, IV therapy, Wound care, Tracheostomy care, Blood draws/Phlebotomy, Catheter insertion/management, Ventilator management, Cardiac monitoring/Telemetry, Pain management, Fall prevention, Infection control, Specimen collection, Patient education, Discharge planning

Technical/EMR

Epic, Cerner, Meditech, CPSI, Allscripts, Electronic health records (EHR), Barcode medication administration (BCMA), Pyxis/Omnicell, Computerized physician order entry (CPOE)

Certifications to list

BLS, ACLS, PALS, TNCC, ENPC, NIH Stroke Scale, CCRN, CEN, RNC-OB, OCN, CNOR, Wound care certification (WCC/CWOCN)

Soft skills that matter in nursing

Unlike most industries, some soft skills are worth listing on a nursing resume because ATS systems screen for them: patient-centered care, interdisciplinary collaboration, care coordination, critical thinking, cultural competency, HIPAA compliance, evidence-based practice.

6. Licenses, certifications, and education

This section belongs near the top of a nursing resume, right after your summary. Format each credential clearly:

Licenses

Registered Nurse (RN), State of California, License #RN 123456, Active
Compact RN License (eNLC), Home state: Texas

Certifications

BLS (American Heart Association), Exp. 2028
ACLS (American Heart Association), Exp. 2027
NIH Stroke Scale, Certified 2025
CCRN (AACN), Exp. 2027
Always include the issuing body and expiration date. Expired certifications should be listed only if you are actively renewing them, and note "Renewal in progress."

Education

List your highest nursing degree first:

Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN)
University of Pennsylvania School of Nursing, Philadelphia, PA
Graduated May 2021 · GPA 3.7 · Magna Cum Laude

Include: Degree, school name, graduation date, GPA if 3.5+, honors. For ADN-to-BSN bridge programs, list both degrees.

Remove: High school, once you have a nursing degree.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Listing duties instead of outcomes: Every nurse "administers medications" and "monitors patients." Your resume needs to show how well you did it and at what scale.
  • Burying your license and certifications: Some nurses put these at the bottom. Recruiters check credentials first. Put them right after your summary.
  • Using a functional or creative format: Hospital ATS systems (Workday, iCIMS, Taleo) expect reverse chronological. Creative designs with graphics and columns break parsing.
  • Omitting your license number: Many hospital HR departments use the license number for immediate verification. Leaving it off creates friction and can delay your application.
  • Generic skills like "compassionate" or "hardworking": These are not searchable by ATS and waste space. Replace them with clinical keywords from the posting.
  • Not tailoring to the unit: An ER job posting and a med-surg posting use different language. If the posting says "fast-paced emergency department," your resume should reflect emergency-specific experience and terminology.
  • Including every clinical rotation or job: Focus on the last 10-15 years. Older roles can be summarized in one line. New grads should include all rotations but trim them to 2-3 bullets each.

Frequently asked questions

Should I put my nursing license number on my resume?

Yes. Include your license type (RN, LPN), state of licensure, and license number. Many hospitals verify this during screening, and ATS systems flag resumes that omit it. If you hold a compact license, note that as well.

How long should a nursing resume be?

One page for nurses with under 10 years of experience. Two pages if you have 10+ years, multiple specialties, or extensive certifications. New grads should always stay on one page.

Should new grad nurses include clinical rotations?

Yes. List each rotation as you would a job: facility name, unit type, dates, and 2-3 bullets describing patient population, skills used, and any outcomes. Clinical rotations are your work experience until you have paid nursing roles to replace them.

Do I need a cover letter for nursing jobs?

For hospital systems using an ATS, the resume matters more. But for smaller clinics, travel nursing agencies, and competitive specialties like NICU or OR, a targeted cover letter helps you stand out. Include one when the application allows it.

What is the best resume format for nurses?

Reverse chronological. It shows your most recent clinical experience first, which is what hiring managers care about most. Avoid functional or skills-based formats because they make it harder for recruiters to see your progression and ATS software struggles to parse them.
